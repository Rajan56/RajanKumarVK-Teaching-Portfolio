01Teaching philosophy

Four principles I teach by

Understanding cannot be handed over; it has to be seen by the student. Each principle below follows from that, and the last one comes from my own experience as a learner: real understanding grows from inquiry and observation, and it needs freedom from fear.

1

Questions before answers

Every topic starts from an open question. Students form their own first view before they meet the literature, so they notice when the evidence changes their mind.

2

Relevance

Students learn best when they see why knowledge matters. I bring real organisational problems and current research on sustainability, measurement and digital transformation into the classroom, as material to question rather than conclusions to accept.

3

Student-centred learning

Students arrive with different backgrounds and strengths. My role is to guide, not to dominate: patient tutoring, constructive feedback and tasks they own.

4

Insight, not accumulation

Learning should not be driven by fear, comparison or pressure. Fear suppresses curiosity. Safety makes exploration possible.

What this looks like in practice

Inquiry over memorisation

Students question assumptions, compare studies that reach different conclusions and ask why, instead of reproducing information.

No fear-based learning

Standards matter, and so does psychological safety. A mistake is part of learning, not a verdict on ability.

Awareness of one's own thinking

Students reflect on how they approach a problem, read data and build an argument, so they learn to correct themselves.

Dialogue as method

Dialogue is shared exploration, not a transfer of authority. I ask before I tell.

Two classrooms, one method

In Nepal I taught fifteen-year-olds with everyday objects and simple experiments. In Finland I mentor university teams working with company managers. The setting changed completely. The method did not change: start from a question the students care about, let them predict, let them test, and only then explain.

How I give feedback

  • Break a complex task into steps a student can act on.
  • Explain what to improve and why it matters.
  • Name strengths, not only errors.
  • Offer another method or viewpoint where it helps.
  • Ask students to reflect on their own reasoning.

Background that shapes my teaching

03A master’s course I would teach

Sustainability in business: measuring and governing it

A planned six-session master’s course built around questions on which research itself disagrees: does sustainability pay, what do indicators hide, who may ask whom for which information, and how can we tell a change in practice from a change in paperwork. Students meet readings that reach different conclusions and learn to ask why.

  • Learning outcomes, activities and assessment aligned to one another
  • Three exercises with fictional data: a business case simulator, a supplier data game and a scenario quiz
  • Clear rules for generative AI, and assessment that rewards reasoning
  • How I would supervise master’s theses and support doctoral research

The opening question

Does sustainability pay?

Sometimes. Sometimes not. The course is about what it depends on, and who gets to decide. Students test the answer themselves with a simple investment model and then compare it with studies that disagree.

04University teaching at LUT

Introduction to Industrial Engineering and Management

From 2023 to 2026 I taught this 3 ECTS course to first-year bachelor students on the Lahti campus: lectures, tutoring, assessment and feedback. First-year students remember what they have done, not what they have heard, so I built the sessions around interaction: concepts students could see, small games, and simple tools that let them discover something about themselves. Three of them are recreated below. Try them.

Learning goal · thinking in processes

Find the bottleneck

A line of four stations. Students first guess how many products per hour the line can finish, then change the capacities and watch. The lesson arrives without a formula: a process is only as fast as its slowest step, and improving anything else is wasted effort.

Line output: 8 products per hour. Bottleneck: Assembly.

Pink dots are work waiting in front of a station. Black dots are products moving along the line.
Learning goal · teamwork and self-reflection

Which team roles come naturally to you?

Before the first group assignment, students rated a few statements about themselves and saw which team roles suit them, and where in working life those strengths are valued. Groups then compared profiles and noticed which roles their team was missing. Move the sliders from "not like me" to "very much like me".

An informal classroom reflection inspired by Belbin's nine team roles. It is not the official Belbin assessment and gives no diagnosis, only a starting point for discussion.

Learning goal · prioritising one’s own work

Urgent or important?

New students often treat everything that is loud as important. In this short game they sort typical tasks of a student week into four quadrants and compare their choices with mine. The discussion afterwards is the real lesson: which quadrant decides a degree?

Read the task on the right and choose a quadrant.

Why tools and games

A first-year group is mixed in background and confidence. A game gives everyone the same starting point, and a tool that says something about the student personally earns attention that a slide cannot.

What I did in the course

Delivered lectures, tutored students individually through assignments and project work, assessed their work and gave feedback. I also assisted and graded on other courses of the department.

A demonstration lecture

For my pedagogy studies I prepared a lecture on the role of digital twins in performance management. It opens with a question to the audience and shows how real-time data, simulation and feedback change the way an organisation sets goals, follows progress and corrects its course.

05Course design

A constructively aligned redesign of a first-year course

As my course planning task in LUT's University Pedagogy studies (submitted 15 May 2026), I redesigned Introduction to Industrial Engineering and Management, a compulsory 3 ECTS blended course for first-year bachelor students. The content was sound. The weakness was coherence: students met the themes as a list of separate tasks. The redesign aligns outcomes, activities and assessment. Select an outcome to see how it is taught and assessed.

Constructive alignment

Outcomes, activities and assessment match, so students know what is worth their time (Biggs & Tang, 2011).

Active learning

Low-threshold, clearly guided activity instead of listening only (Prince, 2004).

Scaffolding

Templates and worked examples early on, with support withdrawn as confidence grows (van de Pol et al., 2010).

Formative feedback

Brief, criteria-linked feedback built into each module (Nicol & Macfarlane-Dick, 2006).

Each module follows a before, during and after rhythm in the blended environment. Assessment instructions state plainly which uses of generative AI are acceptable, and students declare any AI assistance.

06Mentoring in the Lahti Venture Program

Real companies, real problems, mixed teams

The Lahti Venture Program is an industry collaboration course (6 ECTS) at LUT University in which student teams solve a challenge set by a company. Its teams bring together students from different disciplines, so the first lesson is often how to work with someone who thinks differently. I mentored teams in the programme from 2022 to 2026.

Example challenge

Kempower: attracting and keeping Generation Z talent

Kempower, a Finnish maker of electric vehicle charging systems, asked how it could attract and retain employees from Generation Z. The students were themselves that generation, so I began with their own expectations of an employer and turned those into a first hypothesis. From there the team worked in four steps.

  1. Listen. Surveys and interviews with students and young professionals.
  2. Compare. How other technology companies appeal to young talent.
  3. Analyse. Themes, strengths and gaps: purpose, growth, flexibility and openness.
  4. Propose. An employer value proposition for Generation Z, with steps for recruitment, onboarding and retention.

My belief is that teaching should be student-oriented, and mentoring makes that concrete. The ideas belong to the students. My task is to protect the space in which those ideas can appear, and then to give them structure. The same belief guides how I would supervise theses.

07Sustainability in teaching

Three bottom lines in every case

Sustainability is the core of my research, and I do not teach it as a separate chapter. Every case I use is read through the triple bottom line: economic, environmental and social. Below are three teaching designs.

Teaching design 1

The three-column KPI audit

Students take the performance indicators a real organisation reports and sort them into economic, environmental and social columns. The empty spaces are the lesson: what is not measured is not managed. Teams then propose two indicators that would fill the largest gap and justify the data needed.

Teaching design 2

Is there a business case?

Students move energy price, carbon cost, customer reward and regulation, and see when a sustainability investment pays and when it does not. The open question that follows is who should carry the cost when it does not. Try it.

In the first-year course

Sustainability and ethics as an outcome

In my redesign of Introduction to Industrial Engineering and Management, sustainability is written into the intended learning outcomes and assessed through a reflection that links a company visit to the role of the professional.

08Teaching case

From data to decision: a margin recovery case

A fictional Nordic packaging business sees sales grow while its operating margin falls by a quarter. Students work from one integrated dataset to an explanation and a costed plan: a KPI tree, a driver-based bridge of the margin, customer profitability after the cost to serve, a scenario model and a plan whose value is tracked afterwards.

The case also carries a sustainability question. One plant’s energy use drifts upward, and the analysis sizes it in money and in tonnes of CO₂. Is that a cost problem, a sustainability problem, or both? A step on generative AI, with automated number checks and a human who signs off, opens a discussion about accountability.
